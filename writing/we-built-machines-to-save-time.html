We Built Machines to Save Time, and Taught Them to Occupy It

Every generation of communication technology has made messages faster. None of them has made us feel less busy. The pattern is old enough to call a law, and AI is about to test it again.

№ 010June 20268 min readPedrum Shokouhi

Salt Lake CityOctober 24, 1861

On October 24, 1861, workmen in Salt Lake City joined two telegraph wires (one stretching east toward Omaha, one west toward Sacramento), and for the first time in human history a message could cross the North American continent faster than anything carrying it. Two days later, the Pony Express announced it was going out of business. Consider what died that week. The Express was a heroic piece of logistics: nearly two hundred relay stations strung across two thousand miles of desert, mountain, and hostile territory; hundreds of horses; riders, famously young, light, and fearless, who galloped in all weather, handing a mail pouch down a human chain that moved a letter from Missouri to California in ten astonishing days. For eighteen months it was the fastest thing in America. Then two wires touched in Utah, and ten days became ten minutes, and all of it (the stations, the horses, the riders, the entire romantic apparatus) was unnecessary by the weekend. Nobody ever built a faster horse network. The category of problem it solved had simply ceased to exist.

Boston to San FranciscoBy way of Panama, 1850

Now consider the world the telegraph replaced, because it had a virtue we have entirely forgotten how to see. In 1850, a merchant in Boston writing to a partner in San Francisco sat down with paper and ink and composed something built to survive the crossing: the letter would travel by ship to Panama, cross the isthmus by mule and rail, catch another steamer up the Pacific coast, and arrive, if it arrived, in perhaps a month, with a reply not expected for two or three more. This delay would be intolerable to us, but notice what it produced. Words had to be deliberate, because they could not be followed up or walked back. Questions had to matter enough to justify the voyage. And no one on earth could demand an answer from you before another human being had been given time to actually think. The slowness was inconvenient, sometimes agonizing (fortunes and marriages hung on those steamers), but it enforced a boundary that has since vanished from the world: a structural separation between one person’s urgency and another person’s attention. Then the telegraph conquered distance, the telephone made conversation instantaneous, radio and television let one voice reach millions, email deleted the letter’s voyage, messaging deleted email’s remaining ceremony, and the smartphone deleted the last requirement, that you be somewhere in particular, before the world could reach you. Each step was a genuine miracle; separated families spoke, knowledge crossed borders, emergencies met faster responses. But each collapse in transmission time also collapsed the socially acceptable time in which a human being was permitted to respond, and what began as the miracle of immediacy curdled, almost without anyone noticing, into the expectation of availability.

The paradox Keynes didn’t see coming

John Maynard Keynes“Economic Possibilities for our Grandchildren,” 1930
W. S. Jevons · Ruth Schwartz Cowan1865 · 1983

In 1930, with the world sliding into depression, John Maynard Keynes wrote a short essay called “Economic Possibilities for our Grandchildren” in which he made one of the most famously wrong predictions in the history of economics. Compounding productivity growth, he reasoned, would solve the economic problem within a century; his grandchildren’s generation (ours, roughly) would work perhaps fifteen hours a week, and humanity’s real challenge would be learning to fill its oceans of leisure with dignity. The productivity arrived on schedule; an American worker today produces several times what her 1930 counterpart did. The leisure never came. And the reason it never came is the paradox at the heart of modern technology: nearly every machine we build promises to save time, yet almost nobody feels they possess more of it, because society absorbs each efficiency and immediately converts it into a higher expectation. The typewriter did not shorten the clerk’s day; it multiplied the volume of correspondence an office was expected to produce. Email did not eliminate meetings and memos; it multiplied the number of people entitled to put a request in front of you at zero cost to themselves. The washing machine did not reduce hours spent on laundry so much as raise the standard of cleanliness a household was expected to maintain. Economists know the general shape of this as the Jevons paradox (make a resource cheaper to use and total consumption of it rises), and it turns out to govern minutes as ruthlessly as it governs coal. The machines never failed. The expectations simply repriced. I confess I laughed at Keynes the first time I read that essay: the great economist, wrong by a factor of three. Then I opened my own phone’s screen-time report, read it twice, and stopped laughing.

Some friction is where judgment is manufactured.
The Boston merchant1850

The smartphone and the app economy perfected the pattern by converting the saved time into inventory and selling it. There is always another notification to clear, another feed to check, another update to install, another red circle announcing that somewhere, something remains incomplete. No individual interaction costs more than a few seconds, which is what makes the arrangement so difficult to indict: who can object to a glance? But the glances fracture the continuity of thought into fragments too short for anything difficult to grow in, and they have inverted the morning: we begin the day not by deciding what deserves our attention but by auditing what the device accumulated while we slept, mistaking responsiveness for responsibility and motion for progress. These are my own mornings, and I suspect they are yours. The numbers describe a civilization that would be unrecognizable to the Boston merchant: the average American adult now spends somewhere near eight hours a day with digital media, checks a phone dozens upon dozens of times between waking and sleeping, and receives more distinct demands on attention before breakfast than his ancestor received in a season.

What we’re actually delegating

Into this exhausted landscape walks the AI agent, offering itself as the cure. If applications fragmented our lives, the agent promises to reunify them: it will read the messages, triage the inbox, organize the calendar, conduct the research, schedule the meeting, and perhaps eventually attend the meeting in our place, absorbing into itself the administrative silt that has settled over modern life. Instead of ricocheting between interfaces, we will state an intention and await a result. Properly built, this could be one of the most humane improvements in personal computing, because it could abolish entire categories of trivial coordination that should never have consumed human attention in the first place. Before we celebrate, look at what is being handed over. An ordinary machine performs an action; an intelligent agent interprets an intention, and interpretation is where the power lives. Ask a system to “deal with my schedule,” and it must silently decide which commitments matter, which people can be postponed, and how much of your day deserves to remain empty. Ask it to “tell me what I need to know,” and it must decide which events are significant and which sources are trustworthy, which is to say, it must hold opinions about the world on your behalf. Ask it to “make this email sound better,” and it begins, message by message, to shape how you present yourself to other human beings. The agent saves the time a decision costs. Increasingly, it also takes part in the decision itself, and this transfer happens inside a single person, invisibly, with every delegation feeling individually reasonable.

The machines never failed. The expectations simply repriced.

So the goal cannot be the frictionless life. Some friction is where judgment is manufactured. Writing the difficult message yourself forces you to discover what you actually believe, a discovery that reading an AI draft of your position will never produce. Comparing options forces you to confront a trade-off and thereby learn your own preferences, which were not fully formed until the comparison hurt. Reading an argument rather than its summary exposes you to the structure of another mind’s reasoning, not merely its conclusions. I drafted parts of this essay with an AI’s help, and I felt the seduction immediately. The machine’s sentence is always available, always adequate, and accepting it is always easier than excavating my own. Some passages I took back and rewrote because they mattered too much to delegate; some I did not. Whether that makes this essay evidence for its own argument or a quiet betrayal of it is a question I have decided to leave standing in public. Even boredom, that condition our devices have hunted nearly to extinction, has a function: it is the unoccupied mental space in which thoughts nobody selected for you (not an editor, not an algorithm, not an agent) can finally surface. A perfectly frictionless existence would be efficient and shallow: a life administered rather than lived. The divide that matters in the coming decade, then, will not run between people who use AI and people who refuse it; the refusers will be as rare and as quaint as households that declined electricity. It will run between those who delegate execution while retaining judgment and those who, one reasonable convenience at a time, allow the judgment to travel with the task. The essential skill of the age will be knowing when the machine’s answer is not good enough, when a task deserves your personal attention precisely because it is inefficient, and when efficiency itself is solving the wrong problem.

The Pony Express · Keynes1861 · 1930

So yes, we should welcome, gratefully, machines that return time to us; there is real drudgery in every inbox, and no dignity is lost when it dies. But the Pony Express should hang above the door as a warning about how fast an entire architecture of effort can become unnecessary, and Keynes should hang beside it as a warning about what happens next, because his error was not arithmetic but anthropological: he assumed that when the hours were liberated, we would keep them. We did not keep them. We refilled them, instantly, with whatever the newest machine made abundant. If every hour the agents save is immediately reinvested in more messages, more content, more transactions, and more measurable output, we will have repeated the twentieth century’s bargain at a grander scale: an unfathomably efficient civilization whose inhabitants feel perpetually behind. By finally doing what machines do well, AI might return us to what they cannot: staying with a difficult question past the point of comfort, caring for another person at the tempo care actually requires, making something without knowing whether it will succeed, and experiencing an hour without the slightest urge to optimize it. The machines are about to hand us back an enormous quantity of time. No agent can tell us what we intend to do with it. I do not have an answer yet.

00 Hi, Friend

You’re inside the study now.

This is what I study when I am not writing: founders read as case files, investors read like philosophers, six hundred years of people who paid for the future, and the paintings that taught me more about conviction than any pitch deck.

I’m Pedrum. I’ve built companies and just finished an MBA. Almost everything I believe came from people who built things, people who funded them, and paintings I did not understand until suddenly I did. This room holds all three, plus the reading that feeds them.

Nothing here is comprehensive. A founder, a book, or a painting makes the cut only if it changed how I work. Some things are hidden; the colophon has hints.

Pedrum Shokouhi.

01

Twenty-five builders at the fork in the road

Twenty-five builders born between 1835 and 1990, from Rockefeller at sixteen with a ledger to the Collisons with seven lines of code. Each file ends with what I took from it. Open all twenty-five and the drawer gives up its secret.

Files opened: 0 / 25

Portraits from Wikimedia Commons, public domain and CC. Each file carries its credit; the colophon lists them all.

F-01Andrew Carnegie1835–1919 · Carnegie Steel

A thirteen-year-old telegraph messenger who learned to read the wire by sound, then rode the railroads, then bet everything on the Bessemer process while rivals called steel a fad. His mills ran on obsessive cost sheets; he cared less what a ton sold for than what it cost, because cost was the number he controlled. In 1901 he sold to Morgan for $480 million and then executed the stranger half of his plan: giving nearly all of it away on a system, funding more than 2,500 libraries so the next messenger boy could read his way up too.

What I take The exit is the midpoint. What you do with the second half is the biography.

Theodore C. Marceau, Andrew Carnegie.Public domain (opens in a new tab)

Next file: John D. Rockefeller→

F-02John D. Rockefeller1839–1937 · Standard Oil

Before he owned the oil business he owned its arithmetic. As a sixteen-year-old assistant bookkeeper he treated the ledger like scripture, and he never stopped. Rivals guessed at their refining costs; Rockefeller knew his to the third decimal, per barrel, per barrel-hoop, per drop of solder on a can lid. In an industry running on rumor, he was certain about his costs. That certainty let him price rivals into selling and railroads into secret rebates.

What I take Knowing your numbers colder than anyone else is a weapon.

Scientific American Compiling Department, John D. Rockefeller, 1895.Public domain (opens in a new tab)

←Previous file: Andrew CarnegieNext file: Estée Lauder→

F-03Estée Lauder1906–2004 · Estée Lauder

She could not afford advertising, so she gave the product away: a lipstick into a stranger’s hand, a free touch-up at the counter, a sample in every bag whether you bought or not. The industry called it ruinous. Lauder was building a distribution system out of generosity. Every woman who walked out with a miniature sold the brand at her own dinner table, and the gift with purchase she pioneered is now standard across cosmetics. It began as the strategy of someone too poor for billboards.

What I take When you can’t outspend, out-give.

Bill Sauro, New York World-Telegram and the Sun, Estée Lauder, 1966.Public domain (opens in a new tab)

←Previous file: John D. RockefellerNext file: Henry Ford→

F-04Henry Ford1863–1947 · Ford

The moving assembly line cut the time to build a Model T from about twelve hours to about ninety minutes, and the price fell year after year while wages went the other way: in 1914 Ford stunned the industry by roughly doubling pay to five dollars a day. Moralists called it charity and rivals called it madness. Turnover collapsed, the best mechanics in America lined up at his gate, and his own workers became his customers. He understood that a mass market has to be paid into existence.

What I take Speed comes from removing motion.

Fred Hartsook, Henry Ford, 1919.Public domain (opens in a new tab)

←Previous file: Estée LauderNext file: Coco Chanel→

F-05Coco Chanel1883–1971 · Chanel

An orphanage seamstress who rebuilt women’s clothing out of jersey, a humble knit then used mostly for men’s underwear, because it was cheap, and because women could finally move in it. She borrowed from menswear, cut away ornament, and made simplicity a luxury. The cautionary chapter matters too: in 1924 she signed away ninety percent of her perfume business to partners in a deal she spent decades fighting, and only renegotiated her way to a fortune in 1947. She invented modern luxury and nearly gave away its best asset at the same desk.

What I take Subtraction can be a revolution. Read the deal twice, especially when the product is your own name.

Los Angeles Times, Coco Chanel in Los Angeles, 1931.CC BY 4.0 (opens in a new tab)

←Previous file: Henry FordNext file: Walt Disney→

F-06Walt Disney1901–1966 · Disney

Hollywood called Snow White “Disney’s Folly”: a feature-length cartoon that took about three years, ran far over budget, and was financed by a bet-the-studio bank loan. It became the highest-grossing film of its era. In the 1950s the banks laughed at an amusement park, so he borrowed against his own life insurance to start Disneyland, then rewrote the company’s vocabulary around it: employees became cast members, customers became guests, shifts became the show. He understood that language is architecture. Rename the work and you change how it’s done.

What I take Build the world, and name things like the names matter.

Harris & Ewing, Walt Disney with a Mickey Mouse drawing, 1931.Public domain (opens in a new tab)

←Previous file: Coco ChanelNext file: Sam Walton→

F-07Sam Walton1918–1992 · Walmart

He flew a secondhand plane low over county roads to count cars in competitors’ parking lots, walked every rival’s aisles with a notebook, and stood in his own doorways asking customers what he’d gotten wrong. Everyone discounted. Walton’s edge was refusing to let any layer of management stand between him and ground truth. Data flowed up to Bentonville every Saturday morning while his competitors waited for quarterly reports, so Walmart turned its inventory faster than anyone could copy.

What I take Consensus is a lagging indicator. Parking lots are a leading one.

George Bush Presidential Library and Museum, Sam Walton, 1992.Public domain (opens in a new tab)

←Previous file: Walt DisneyNext file: Akio Morita→

F-08Akio Morita1921–1999 · Sony

In 1955 Bulova offered to buy a hundred thousand of Sony’s transistor radios, an order bigger than the company itself, on one condition: Bulova’s name on the case. Morita, whose country’s exports were still a punchline for shoddiness, said no. He would not sell the name, the one asset that outlasts every product. Decades of Trinitrons and Walkmans later, “Made in Japan” meant precision, and that single refusal did as much as any product to change it.

What I take Never sell the compounding asset to finance the depreciating one.

Avelino Ginjo, Akio Morita in São Paulo, 1972.Public domain (opens in a new tab)

←Previous file: Sam WaltonNext file: Ingvar Kamprad→

F-09Ingvar Kamprad1926–2018 · IKEA

A dyslexic farm boy who started by selling matches from a bicycle, then seeds, then pens, then furniture by catalog. The founding accident became doctrine in 1956 when a colleague sawed the legs off a table to fit it in a car: flat packing moved assembly, shipping, and warehousing onto the customer, and IKEA turned the difference into permanently lower prices. Kamprad flew economy, drove an old Volvo, and wrote frugality into A Furniture Dealer’s Testament. He understood that cost discipline is a culture, and a culture is the only cost program that outlives its author.

What I take Every cost you remove is pricing power you keep forever. Inconvenience is a business model.

Sandra Baqirjazid, Swedish Ministry of Enterprise, Energy and Communications, Ingvar Kamprad, Haparanda, 2010.CC BY 2.0 (opens in a new tab)

←Previous file: Akio MoritaNext file: Soichiro Honda→

F-10Soichiro Honda1906–1991 · Honda

Toyota rejected his first batch of piston rings: years of work, judged scrap. He went back to school for metallurgy, remade them, and won the contract; then war and an earthquake took the factory anyway. He started over with surplus generator engines bolted to bicycles. What never changed was the order of his loves: the machine first, the market second. He tuned engines by ear, and in 1954 he announced Honda would race the Isle of Man TT; the team arrived in 1959 and won in 1961. The market forgave every setback because the machines kept getting better.

What I take Obsession with craft survives disasters that strategy doesn’t.

Asahi Shimbun, Soichiro Honda, Asahigraph, 1955.Public domain (opens in a new tab)

←Previous file: Ingvar KampradNext file: Morris Chang→

F-11Morris Changb. 1931 · TSMC

Twenty-five years at Texas Instruments, passed over for the top job, and then a founding at an age when most executives write memoirs. Recruited to Taiwan, he started TSMC in 1987 at fifty-five on a model nobody wanted: make everyone’s chips, design none of your own, compete with no customer. The pure-play foundry looked like humble contract work. TSMC now makes most of the world’s most advanced chips.

What I take Being passed over can be the founding event. A new business model beats a new product.

Office of the President, Taiwan, Morris Chang, 2021.CC BY 2.0 (opens in a new tab)

←Previous file: Soichiro HondaNext file: Steve Jobs→

F-12Steve Jobs1955–2011 · Apple, Pixar

Most people know the keynotes. Look at the detours: a calligraphy class with no plan, a firing, and the NeXT and Pixar years. Together they gave Apple typography, an operating system and storytelling. None of it looked like a career. All of it became the moat. Taste accumulates in detours, and Apple’s margins are what taste looks like on an income statement.

What I take The detour you can’t justify today is often the edge you’ll monetize in a decade.

Matthew Yohe, Steve Jobs, 2010.CC BY-SA 3.0 (opens in a new tab)

←Previous file: Morris ChangNext file: Bill Gates→

F-13Bill Gatesb. 1955 · Microsoft

In 1976 he wrote an open letter scolding hobbyists for copying software, insisting that code, though free to duplicate, still deserved a price. That stubborn idea became an industry. The bigger deal came in 1980: IBM needed an operating system, Gates bought one from a small Seattle shop for a modest sum, licensed it to IBM non-exclusively, and kept the right to sell it to every clone maker on earth. IBM got a product; Gates got the standard.

What I take Own the license. The margin lives in the rights.

Bogdan Hoyaux, European Union, Bill Gates, 2025.CC BY 4.0 (opens in a new tab)

←Previous file: Steve JobsNext file: Tim Cook→

F-14Tim Cookb. 1960 · Apple

He joined Apple in 1998, when it was months from irrelevance, and did the unglamorous surgery: closed the factories, outsourced with precision, and drove inventory from months of stock to days, treating logistics with the same fanaticism the design studio reserved for corners and radii. Then he took the job of following a founder-myth and refused to impersonate him. He ran the company as an operator: services, silicon, privacy as policy, and roughly a tenfold rise. Operations, done obsessively, is strategy.

What I take Don’t play the predecessor’s instrument. Master your own.

Dati Bendo, European Union, Tim Cook, 2026.CC BY 4.0 (opens in a new tab)

←Previous file: Bill GatesNext file: Phil Knight→

F-15Phil Knightb. 1938 · Nike

For its first decade, Blue Ribbon Sports was a company perpetually one bank meeting from death. Knight kept his accounting job to make payroll, begged letters of credit, and lived on float. The lesson buried in his memoir is duration. The dark middle of a company lasts years longer than any founding myth admits. The founders who make it arranged their lives so they could keep going while nothing worked.

What I take Structure your life to survive the middle. The middle is most of it.

University of Oregon, Bill Bowerman coaching Phil Knight and teammates, 1958.Public domain (opens in a new tab)

←Previous file: Tim CookNext file: Bernard Arnault→

F-16Bernard Arnaultb. 1949 · LVMH

In 1984 he bought a bankrupt textile conglomerate, mills and diapers and department stores, because buried in the wreckage sat one asset the accountants had no line for: Christian Dior. He sold most of the rest. The insight that built LVMH is that a great name is stored desire, and desire has software economics: near-zero marginal cost, pricing power that rises with scarcity, and no expiry date if maintained. He then applied the discipline luxury lacked, real logistics and real balance sheets, without ever letting the customer see the machinery.

What I take Inside every distressed asset, look for the one thing that compounds. Buy that; sell the rest.

Jérémy Barande, Bernard Arnault, 2017.CC BY-SA 2.0 (opens in a new tab)

←Previous file: Phil KnightNext file: Jeff Bezos→

JBF-17Jeff Bezosb. 1964 · Amazon

The 1997 shareholder letter reads like a dare: we will be misunderstood for long periods, we will price for market leadership rather than quarterly optics, and we will make bold bets where the odds are good but the outcome uncertain. Then he reprinted it behind every letter that followed, an annual public contract with his own time horizon. Everything distinctive about Amazon, from AWS growing out of internal plumbing to a decade of “unprofitability” spent on reinvestment, falls out of one variable set differently: the clock.

What I take Most competition dissolves the moment you change the time horizon.

←Previous file: Bernard ArnaultNext file: Jensen Huang→

F-18Jensen Huangb. 1963 · NVIDIA

NVIDIA nearly died in 1996; Huang laid off most of the company and bet the remains on a single chip. Then came roughly a decade building CUDA, a programming platform for computation nobody was asking for, while Wall Street treated it as the expensive eccentricity of a video-game company. When deep learning arrived, the only shovels in the store were his. He explained the bet constantly. People found it boring to believe him.

What I take You can announce your decade-long bet out loud. Almost nobody will bother to front-run conviction.

Anderseidesvik, Jensen Huang at Stanford, 2026.CC BY-SA 4.0 (opens in a new tab)

←Previous file: Jeff BezosNext file: Elon Musk→

F-19Elon Muskb. 1971 · SpaceX, Tesla

The founding argument was arithmetic: a rocket’s raw materials cost a small fraction of its sticker price, so the price was a habit of the industry. Rivals that had priced in throwing the machine away ridiculed reusability. The stress test came in 2008. On September 28, after three failures, SpaceX’s fourth Falcon 1 reached orbit. On December 23 NASA awarded SpaceX a space-station cargo contract, and on Christmas Eve Tesla’s rescue financing closed. The method: price the physics, and size your commitment so survival itself is the moat.

What I take First principles are a demolition tool. Use them on the invoice everyone else accepts.

Gage Skidmore, Elon Musk, 2025.CC BY-SA 4.0 (opens in a new tab)

←Previous file: Jensen HuangNext file: Larry Page & Sergey Brin→

F-20Larry Page & Sergey Brinb. 1973 · Google

Two graduate students who treated the web like academia and links like citations: a page mattered if pages that mattered pointed at it. PageRank put a library-science idea behind a search box. The part I keep is the humiliation: in 1999 they tried to sell the whole thing to Excite for under a million dollars, and were turned down. The market’s no forced them to build the company instead, and the ad auction they later bolted onto search paid for everything that followed.

What I take Sometimes the market refuses you into greatness. Keep a list of your rejections; one of them is a gift.

Stansfield PL, Larry Page, 2009.; Steve Jurvetson, Sergey Brin, 2010.CC BY-SA 3.0 (opens in a new tab) · CC BY 2.0 (opens in a new tab)

←Previous file: Elon MuskNext file: Tobi Lütke→

F-21Tobi Lütkeb. 1980 · Shopify

He built an online snowboard shop, hated every e-commerce tool available, and wrote his own. The snowboards were the false start; the realization that the tooling was the company was the founding. Shopify’s strategy has stayed weirdly consistent ever since: arm the rebels. Give a million small merchants the infrastructure of a giant and take a sliver of everything that flows through. It’s the rare platform-era company whose incentives point the same direction as its customers’.

What I take The tool you built out of frustration for yourself is market research.

Benjamin Forrest, Tobi Lütke, 2019.CC BY-SA 4.0 (opens in a new tab)

←Previous file: Larry Page & Sergey BrinNext file: Zhang Yiming→

ZYF-22Zhang Yimingb. 1983 · ByteDance

Every Western platform assumed the social graph was the product: you see what your friends share. Zhang bet the graph was the bottleneck, and that a recommendation engine reading behavior directly could out-program your friends within a week of watching you. TikTok is that bet at planetary scale, and Western feeds copied it. I watch him the way one watches weather. My essay on machines occupying time is, in part, about what he proved.

What I take Interface decisions are destiny. Whoever chooses what you see next is the real editor-in-chief.

←Previous file: Tobi LütkeNext file: Brian Chesky→

F-23Brian Cheskyb. 1981 · Airbnb

When nobody would fund air mattresses for strangers, he sold novelty election-themed cereal to stay alive, which sounds like a joke until you see what it signals: a founder who will manufacture survival out of anything. The deeper contribution came after. Airbnb sold trust: reviews, verified identities, professional photos, and a host guarantee. Together they made sleeping in a stranger’s home feel safe. He treated trust as a design problem with components.

What I take Trust can be designed and shipped like any other feature.

Celeste Sloman, Brian Chesky, 2025.CC BY-SA 4.0 (opens in a new tab)

←Previous file: Zhang YimingNext file: Patrick & John Collison→

F-24Patrick & John Collisonb. 1988 / 1990 · Stripe

Online payments circa 2010 meant weeks of paperwork with a bank’s gateway partner. Stripe’s founding artifact was seven lines of code a developer could paste before lunch. The Collisons picked the least glamorous layer of the internet, money plumbing, and treated it with the care other companies reserve for logo redesigns: obsessive documentation, APIs as literature, infrastructure as a form of ambition. Then they kept widening the aperture until the company behaved like infrastructure for the GDP of the internet.

What I take Pick the boring layer everyone depends on and make it beautiful. Boring plus beautiful is a monopoly.

JD Lasica, Patrick Collison, 2015.; UK Prime Minister's Office, John Collison, 2023.CC BY 2.0 (opens in a new tab) · CC BY 2.0 (opens in a new tab)

←Previous file: Brian CheskyNext file: James Dyson→

F-25James Dysonb. 1947 · Dyson

Five thousand one hundred and twenty-six failed prototypes of a bagless vacuum, built in a coach house between 1979 and 1984. The appliance giants declined to license a machine that would make their bags obsolete, so he manufactured it himself, kept full ownership, and let the failures become the company’s culture: engineers there are hired to be wrong quickly.

What I take Iteration is a moat because it’s miserable. Count your prototypes and keep going.

The Royal Society, James Dyson, 2015.CC BY-SA 4.0 (opens in a new tab)

Museum of No · 23

02

No is the most expensive word in business

Decca said it to the Beatles, and Kodak said it to its own digital camera. Morita said it to Bulova and built Sony. What separates the two kinds of no is what the refuser understood about time.

Refusals that cost empires

  1. 1962

    Decca Records

    1962 · London

    Auditioned the Beatles on January 1, 1962, and passed. The line attributed to the label: “Guitar groups are on the way out.”

  2. Western Union

    1876 · New York

    Was offered Bell’s telephone patents for $100,000 and declined. The telegraph company judged the telephone a toy, and the toy ate the company.

    $100,000

    Alexander Graham Bell, Telephone patent drawing, 1876 (National Archives).Public domain (opens in a new tab)

  3. Kodak

    1975 · Rochester

    Steve Sasson built the first digital camera in Kodak’s own lab in 1975. Leadership shelved it to protect film. Kodak filed for bankruptcy in 2012.

    Bertrand Labévue, Drawing of Steven Sasson's 1975 Kodak prototype, 2022.CC BY-SA 4.0 (opens in a new tab)

  4. 1999

    Excite

    1999 · Silicon Valley

    Declined to buy Google for under a million dollars. Excite@Home filed for bankruptcy in 2001.

    under $1M

  5. Blockbuster

    2000 · Dallas

    Passed on buying Netflix for $50 million and reportedly found the pitch amusing. Blockbuster filed for bankruptcy in 2010.

    $50M

    Coasterlover1994, The last Blockbuster, Bend, Oregon, 2018.CC BY-SA 4.0 (opens in a new tab)

  6. 1982

    Mars, Inc.

    1982 · Hollywood

    Declined product placement for a small alien with a candy habit. Reese’s Pieces said yes, and sales jumped.

Refusals that built empires

  1. Akio Morita

    1955 · Tokyo

    Refused Bulova’s hundred-thousand-unit order rather than put another company’s name on his radios. Case file F-08 tells the rest.

    100,000 radios

    ブルーノ・プラス, Sony TR-55 transistor radio, 2018.CC0 (opens in a new tab)

  2. 2006

    Mark Zuckerberg

    2006 · Palo Alto

    Turned down Yahoo’s $1 billion at twenty-two, when his board and common sense said take it. Six years later Facebook went public at a $104 billion valuation.

    $1B

  3. 1997

    Steve Jobs

    1997 · Cupertino

    Returned to a dying Apple and killed most of the product line, drawing a two-by-two grid and refusing everything outside it. Focus is a list of funerals.

  4. In-N-Out

    since 1948 · California

    Nearly eighty years of refusing to franchise, to sprawl the menu, or to go public. The no is the brand.

    RightCowLeftCoast, In-N-Out Store No. 1 replica, 2019.CC BY-SA 4.0 (opens in a new tab)

Refusing the rescue

  1. 2008

    Yahoo

    2008 · Sunnyvale

    Refused Microsoft’s $44.6 billion. Within a decade Verizon bought the core business for $4.8 billion.

    $44.6B

  2. 2010

    Groupon

    2010 · Chicago

    Turned down $6 billion from Google at the peak of daily-deal mania, then watched ninety percent of its value evaporate after the IPO. Size your certainty before you size your no.

    $6B

  3. 2003

    Friendster

    2003 · Silicon Valley

    Declined Google’s $30 million in stock, worth billions today, to stay independent. Then it lost the category to a Harvard dorm room.

    $30M

Refusing the crowd

  1. 1990s

    Bill Watterson

    1990s · Ohio

    Refused every Calvin and Hobbes license: no plush tigers, no film, no cereal. He walked away from a fortune estimated in the hundreds of millions. Some assets keep their value by staying unsold.

  2. EF

    Enzo Ferrari

    Maranello · doctrine

    The house rule attributed to the old man: always sell one car fewer than the market demands. Scarcity was the product.

  3. 1985

    Costco

    since 1985 · Issaquah

    The $1.50 hot dog combo has not moved since 1985. A departing chief executive reportedly told his successor that raising it would be a firing offense. A price can be a promise.

    $1.50

  4. 2010

    Steve Jobs

    2010 · Cupertino

    Published “Thoughts on Flash,” a public letter refusing Flash on the iPhone, while much of the industry called it arrogance. Adobe ended Flash in 2020.

Refusing the spotlight

  1. 1957

    Cy Twombly

    1957–2011 · Rome

    Gave almost no interviews across half a century and let the work speak. The paintings aged better than any explanation would have.

  2. 1964

    Harper Lee

    after 1964 · Monroeville

    Won the Pulitzer Prize for To Kill a Mockingbird in 1961, then declined nearly every interview for fifty years. The silence protected the book from its author, and the author from the noise.

  3. 2011

    Satoshi Nakamoto

    2011 · nowhere

    Built a system later worth more than a trillion dollars, then walked away from roughly a million untouched bitcoins and from the name itself. A network with no founder to trust was the design.

    ~1M bitcoins

Standing refusals

  1. M&C

    Mars & Cargill

    a century and counting

    Two of America’s largest companies, private since their founding. Time horizon is the one advantage a shareholder letter cannot grant.

  2. 1996

    Valve

    since 1996 · Bellevue

    No IPO, no acquisition, barely an org chart, while running Steam, one of software’s great money machines. Staying illegible is a strategy.

  3. 2022

    Patagonia

    2022 · Ventura

    Yvon Chouinard gave the company to a trust and a nonprofit and declared Earth its only shareholder. The last no, said to cashing out.

  1. Lineage of Capital · 9

    03

    The Renaissance had a cap table

    Behind Donatello’s bronze David stands a banker. Behind the computer age stands a professor with a new kind of fund. Nine financiers in six hundred years, each handing the next a tool that did not exist before, and a tenth place still open.

  2. 1389
    1389–1464 · Florence

    Cosimo de’ Medici

    The bank made the money, and Cosimo spent it on Florence. He backed Brunelleschi and Donatello the way a modern fund seeds moonshots: diversified bets on unproven genius, held through ridicule, with returns paid in a changed city.

    Handed down · capital as a lever on culture

    Pontormo, Portrait of Cosimo il Vecchio, about 1519.Public domain (opens in a new tab)

  3. 1459
    1459–1525 · Augsburg

    Jakob Fugger

    He financed the 1519 election of Charles V as Holy Roman Emperor, priced sovereign risk when the concept barely existed, and ran a courier network faster than any crown’s. In 1516 he founded the Fuggerei, housing for the poor that still stands. The rent is still one Rhenish guilder a year, about 0.88 euros.

    Handed down · information advantage as the core asset

    Albrecht Dürer, Portrait of Jakob Fugger, about 1519.Public domain (opens in a new tab)

  4. 1744
    1744–1812 · Frankfurt

    Mayer Amschel Rothschild

    From a ghetto coin shop he placed five sons in five cities: Frankfurt, London, Paris, Vienna, and Naples. The family’s couriers carried market-moving news ahead of governments. The structure itself, a distributed partnership with shared intelligence, was the innovation.

    Handed down · the network as the fund

    Moritz Daniel Oppenheim, The Elector of Hesse Entrusting Mayer Amschel Rothschild with His Treasure.Public domain (opens in a new tab)

  5. 1837
    1837–1913 · New York

    J. Pierpont Morgan

    In the panic of 1907, with no central bank in existence, one private citizen locked the heads of New York’s banks in his library until they pledged the capital to stop the run. He financed Edison, consolidated railroads and steel, and showed capital’s strangest power: to be, for a moment, the state.

    Handed down · the backstop

    Unknown photographer, J. P. Morgan, 1902.Public domain (opens in a new tab)

  6. 1894
    1894–1976 · New York

    Benjamin Graham

    After the crash nearly ruined him, he wrote the discipline down. Security Analysis, published in 1934, turned investing into a craft with a textbook instead of a temperament. His Columbia classroom seeded the industry, and his best investment was a student from Omaha.

    Handed down · the method, written down and taught

    See allocator file A-01→

    Unknown photographer, Benjamin Graham, 1950.Public domain (opens in a new tab)

  7. 1899
    1899–1987 · Boston

    Georges Doriot

    He took Graham’s discipline for buying existing value and pointed it at companies that did not exist yet. ARD, founded in 1946, was the first publicly owned venture capital firm, DEC its proof, and his Harvard students his real portfolio.

    Handed down · institutional money for the unbuilt

    See allocator file A-10→

What would you pay for?

Every investor answers one question: what do you believe enough to pay for? Here are ten answers. Graham nearly lost everything in 1929 and turned the scar into a method; Simons banned storytelling from the building.

Margin of safety is humility expressed in arithmetic.

01 / 10

Benjamin Graham

1894–1976
Graham-Newman
In the lineage, No. 5→

He was brilliant before 1929 and nearly wiped out anyway; the crash took most of his partnership’s capital while he watched. What he wrote down afterward became Security Analysis. Buy so far below conservative value that being wrong is survivable, treat the market as a manic counterparty rather than an oracle, and never confuse a good company with a good price.

U.S. International Trade Administration, Warren Buffett, 2015.Public domain (opens in a new tab)

Inactivity is a position, and most of the time it’s the correct one.

02 / 10

Warren Buffett

b. 1930
Berkshire Hathaway

The public knows the compounding; the professionals study the sitting. Buffett’s edge is temperament: holding cash for years while being called a has-been (1969, 1999), then acting in size within days when the odds finally tilt. He tells students to imagine a punch card with twenty lifetime slots. The scarcity is the discipline. Almost everything, including almost everything good, has to be let go.

Nick, Charlie Munger, 2010.CC BY 2.0 (opens in a new tab)

Invert, always invert. The exits are marked more clearly than the entrances.

03 / 10

Charlie Munger

1924–2023
Berkshire Hathaway

A lawyer who read physics, biology, and psychology and refused to accept that finance had its own sealed logic. His latticework method hangs every decision on the big models of many disciplines and lets them argue. It is a defense system against the brain’s shortcuts, which he catalogued as twenty-five causes of human misjudgment. His favorite operation was inversion: don’t ask how to succeed. Ask what guarantees ruin, then avoid it.

kellywritershouse, Howard Marks, 2012.CC BY 2.0 (opens in a new tab)

You can’t predict. You can prepare.

04 / 10

Howard Marks

b. 1946
Oaktree

His memos rest on one admission: you cannot know the future, but you can know where you stand. Marks reads markets like weather, never predicting the storm’s hour but noticing when everyone has stopped carrying umbrellas. First-level thinking says a great company is a great stock; second-level thinking asks what is already in the price. The pendulum between greed and fear never stops. His method is knowing which way it is swinging.

PL29%

The instrument doesn’t make the return. The holder’s behavior does.

05 / 10

Peter Lynch

b. 1944
Fidelity Magellan

Thirteen years running Magellan at roughly 29% a year. The detail that haunts me is the finding, often repeated, that many of his own investors still lost money, because they piled in after hot streaks and fled after dips. The fund compounded; the behavior didn’t. His method was almost insultingly humble: know what you own, be able to explain it to a ten-year-old in two minutes, and notice what is in your own cart before Wall Street does.

Aris Oikonomou, European Commission, George Soros, 2017.CC BY 4.0 (opens in a new tab)

Markets are participants in the reality they price.

06 / 10

George Soros

b. 1930
Quantum Fund

He wanted to be a philosopher. Reflexivity, his idea that markets write reality as well as read it, because rising prices change the fundamentals that justify them, is a contribution to epistemology that happened to pay. In 1992, with conviction and setup aligned, he bet ten billion dollars against the Bank of England and broke the sterling peg. The philosophy chose the trade; the sizing made it history.

SD30%

Conviction without sizing is just opinion.

07 / 10

Stanley Druckenmiller

b. 1953
Duquesne

Thirty years of roughly 30% annually and, by his account, no down year. The record rests on asymmetry of expression: tiny when uncertain, enormous when the setup is clear, and out at once when the thesis cracks. He credits Soros with the lesson that separates professionals from talented amateurs: what matters is how much you make when right and how much you lose when wrong.

Gleuschk, Jim Simons at MSRI, 2007.CC BY-SA 3.0 (opens in a new tab)

The market owes your story nothing.

08 / 10

Jim Simons

1938–2024
Renaissance

A prize-winning geometer who walked away from mathematics, hired physicists and codebreakers instead of MBAs, and banned narrative from the building: if the signal is real, trade it. Nobody needs to explain why. The Medallion fund’s returns, decades of them, are an uncomfortable dataset for everyone else in finance, because they suggest the stories most investors trade on are mostly decoration. I keep him around as a standing rebuke: maybe the narrative is the bug.

DS

Time horizon is an asset class. So is working on something you love.

09 / 10

David Swensen

1954–2021
Yale Endowment

He left Wall Street for a university job that paid a fraction of what he was worth, then rewrote institutional investing: out of bonds, into venture, private equity, timber, and absolute return, harvesting the premium the impatient leave on the table because a university thinks in centuries. The Yale Model made billions for education, and his protégés went on to run endowments at Princeton, MIT, Stanford and others. What I underline is the career: he priced meaning into the compensation.

GD1946

Back the person; the company is the byproduct.

10 / 10

Georges Doriot

1899–1987
ARD
In the lineage, No. 6→

A Harvard professor who institutionalized a new asset class: in 1957 his American Research & Development put about $70,000 into two engineers called Digital Equipment and watched it become $355 million, the first proof that a venture fund could work. His doctrine was stranger than the returns. He said he was building men and companies, in that order, and held positions for decades.

The Blackboard

11

After Twombly. Leave a mark.

In the late sixties Twombly painted loops of white line on slate-dark grounds, handwriting with the language removed. Here is a small board of your own, with white chalk, orange chalk, a date stamp, and a wipe. Nothing is saved unless you keep it.

Kept on this device, never uploaded

Colophon

12

How this was made

Type
Set in Georgia for reading and Helvetica for labels.
Color
One orange, #FF4D00, marks the P.S. and whatever you have selected or switched on.
Made
Written by hand in HTML, CSS and JavaScript, with the 3D drawn directly in WebGL. No framework, no library, no tracker, no cookie banner, no analytics.
Hosting
Served from Cloudflare’s edge.
Credits
Influences, borrowed openly. Jony Ive, for restraint as a feature. Virgil Abloh, for the license to show your influences. KAWS, for refusing to choose between art and commerce. Cy Twombly, for the chalk. And the essay culture of a certain orange startup school.

      Laying out the case files.

      Experiment 14 · The boardroom · Case, about 15 min

      Final round

      Four cases from a strategy-firm final round. Find the number that matters, say what it means, and stop.

      14
      Brief

      The cases: a cinema chain whose profit fell while revenue rose, a European grocer weighing the American South, a buyout fund with a week to price a chain of animal hospitals, and a startup with no idea what to charge. Play the question back, build the issue tree, pull the exhibits, do the math in your head and give the answer first. You are scored on structure, analytics, judgment and communication, with a reason for every score. The firm, the clients and the case numbers are fictional. The public facts in each case file are real.

      Setting up the interview room.

      The interviewer is grading one habit: the answer first, then the math that holds it up.

                        XI
                        Experiment 42 · The foundry · Simulation, about 12 min

                        The titan’s ledger

                        Play Rockefeller from a Cleveland refinery in 1868 to the Supreme Court in 1911, or Carnegie from his 1872 bet on steel to the 1901 sale to J.P. Morgan. The machine keeps two ledgers, and only one is money.

                        42
                        Brief

                        Almost every crossroads is real and sourced: the secret rebates, the Cleveland buyout, the pipelines, the trust, Homestead, the Mesabi ore and the sale. The levers are the ones they pulled: cost, scale, price, integration, rebates, labor and law. The roads they did not take are open too.

                        Opening the ledgers.

                        Kerosene fell to about six cents a gallon and steel rails to under $20 a ton. Every line in the other ledger is also true, and this machine keeps both books open.

                        Experiment 43 · The foundry · Simulation, about 15 min

                        The batch

                        Y Combinator gives each company $500,000 on two SAFEs, three months and one number to watch: weekly growth. Pick an idea and a cofounder, survive 12 weeks, raise after Demo Day, and find out whether you are default alive.

                        43
                        Brief

                        Each week you choose between talking to users and building, launching ugly and launching late, the early check and Demo Day. The program facts and the dilution math are YC’s own and sourced. Every company and person in it is fiction. Make something people want, and do the arithmetic before you sign.

                        Clearing a desk for the batch.

                        Twelve weeks is long enough to build the wrong thing well, and long enough to find ten people who would be very disappointed if you vanished. Demo Day grades only the second.

                        Wheeling in the machines.

                        Experiment 45 · The foundry · Simulation, about 20 min

                        Frontier: the scaling bet

                        Run Aldermoor, a fictional AI lab one model behind the frontier, with $5.0B and 40,000 GPUs. Eight half-years.

                        Brief

                        The levers are the industry’s own: compute for the training run or for research that makes the next run cheaper, a bigger model or a leaner one that is cheaper to serve, which compute contracts to sign, what to charge per million tokens, how much safety work each capability level demands, and whose money to take. Staying at the frontier is a spending decision. Staying trustworthy there is a series of them. The lab, its rivals and every number are fiction. The case file keeps the real history of OpenAI, Anthropic and the rest separate, sourced and neutral.

                        Booking the compute.

                        Ten times the compute buys the same step, every time. The scaling law never says who pays for the tenfold, or what you promised on the way.

                          Experiment 46 · The last mile · Simulation, about 20 min

                          Two seventy

                          Two invented parties, the real 538-elector map. Nine weeks, a finite war chest, polls that can miss together. You need 270, not a majority.

                          Brief

                          The map has 538 electors, apportioned by the 2020 census. You need 270, and Maine and Nebraska split theirs by district. Your candidate can be in three places a week. Persuasion fades in days; turnout takes weeks to build. Survive the debates and the October story, then sit through an election night where the early lead mostly tells you who counted first. The parties, candidates and map politics are fiction. The rules, the arithmetic and the history in the case file are real.

                          Printing the map.

                          The electoral college asks where your voters live, one state at a time. The first ticket to 270 wins.

                          Experiment 47 · The last mile · Simulation, about 20 min

                          The living drug

                          CAR-T is a living drug: a patient’s own T cells, collected, flown to a factory, given a new receptor, grown and returned. Choose the target, design the receptor, build the factory, run the trial and set the price.

                          47
                          Brief

                          In the phase 1 trial, the toxicity you cause comes from the same biology as the cure. Every lever is a trade-off that real design reviews argue about, and none has an obvious answer. Educational only. This is not medical advice, the numbers in the model are labeled assumptions, and nothing here should inform a decision about a real person.

                          Warming the incubator.

                          A pill is a molecule. This drug is alive and runs on a delivery schedule, and the schedule is what usually kills the program.
